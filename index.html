Annotations

On the syllabus, each week and many respective readings are annotated for their form and function. Annotations appear in tinted blocks — week-level learning narratives at full width, reading-level notes nested beneath their citations. They document the course’s design rationale and would not appear on the student-facing syllabus.

Who is this syllabus for?

This syllabus is designed for two possible uses. Firstly, as an upper-level undergraduate course, particularly for students interested in careers broadly concerned with or impacting the lives of young people, be that policy, afterschool work, classroom teaching, nonprofit/family services, etc. Alternatively, as an elective course in a professional master’s degree program, for example, offered at a graduate school of education or at a graduate school of social policy and practice.

Readings Selection and Adaptability

I selected the readings on this syllabus for their pedagogical leverage, their accessibility, or their utility as primary sources for critical reflection. The same intention governs how the readings appear: where I assign excerpts, I have hand-curated them, trimming each text to the pages that do the specific work I assigned it to do. None of this is fixed. The reading load can expand or contract with the course’s format and audience, and with more development time — certainly in preparing to actually teach the course — I would replace some texts altogether with media, video, or designed activities that make the same contribution while varying what class time feels like.

The syllabus also provides an intended sequence for the readings. That sequence follows from the learning experience and goals, and from the ways each reading shapes how students will meet the next. I have sequenced them to build engagement, keep the material within reach, and opportune critical reflexivity at the moments students are readiest for it.

Learning Goals

I have baked the following learning goals into the course’s design, description, and structure.

1Understand childhood as something socially produced

Students come to recognize childhood as a historical and cultural construction, and they learn to name the frameworks and identify assumptions that naturalize it. By working through developmental psychology, socialization, adultism, and the colonial and pre-colonial history of the child, students gain a vocabulary for childhood and an ability to take that vocabulary apart, tracing each idea back to the arrangements of power that hold it in place.

STUDENTS WILL BE ABLE TO
›
Define and correctly use core concepts in the field of childhood studies. (Foundational Knowledge)
›
Describe how a given framework positions the child, and identify the underlying assumptions and characteristic vocabulary in applied contexts. (Foundational Knowledge)
›
Analyze a primary source text and surface the assumptions about children embedded in it. (Application: critical thinking)
2Read Closely, Identify Tensions, and Stake Positions

Much of the middle section of the course places readings beside each other and asks students to discover the tension between them on their own. Oftentimes, the tension is between an operative and common-sense assumption or belief, or a material reality, and the week’s conceptual intervention. Students build a habit of reading across texts, locating the assumptions beneath familiar positions and vocabulary for talking about children, and staking a position of their own based on their professional or personal experience. Many readings are selected to push students to think about challenges and possibilities of applying conceptual interventions to real-world circumstances.

STUDENTS WILL BE ABLE TO
›
Read across a pair of texts and name, in their own words, where the texts rub against each other and what is at stake. (Application: critical thinking)
›
Decide what to do with that tension: resolve it, hold its contradictions, subvert one through the other, etc. (Application: practical thinking)
›
Recognize how knowledge about children is made and contested; use that understanding to read new material on their own and set their own questions. (Learning How to Learn)
3Connect the experience of childhood to power, and to students’ own worlds

The course treats childhood as a condition shaped by, and productive of, overlapping structures of oppression: race, colonialism, dispossession, labor, capitalism. Students learn to trace these connections across the semester, linking one week’s concept to the next and connecting the course’s ideas to what they see in their own families, communities, workplaces, and volunteer settings. The course depends on students bringing that experience into the room; the goal is for them to read their own lives and the wider social world through what they study, and to study through what they live.

STUDENTS WILL BE ABLE TO
›
Show how experiences of childhood are entangled with arrangements of race, gender, and power, tracing that entanglement across multiple levels (historical formation, material realities, cultural production, and conceptual formulation).
›
Relate course ideas to their own observations and lived experience, and bring those connections into discussion and writing. (Integration)
›
Listen to and understand experiences of childhood different from their own, and engage classmates’ contributions as part of how they think. (Human Dimension: others)
4Learn from what children do, and let it change how we think

The final third of the course focuses squarely on children’s experiences of childhood, what they do within these various conditions (their work, care, play, movement, relationships, etc.) and interrogates what the words we use for children’s practices do. Students learn to use children’s experiences and practices as a test of the concepts we use to make sense of the world. Students attend to what our epistemologies occlude and foreclose, and what openings and ruptures children’s practices reveal.

STUDENTS WILL BE ABLE TO
›
Examine an account of children’s practices and assess what a given word for it does politically (Application: critical thinking)
›
Draw on what children do to imagine different social arrangements and futures, including potential conditions for collective liberation. (Application: creative thinking)
›
Use accounts of children’s lives to identify how the shape of modern childhood impacts what the people consigned to it can, cannot, and must do. (Integration)
5Turn beliefs and knowledge into praxis

Across the semester, students translate their reading and discussion into a running set of carefully worded statements of what they believe about childhood and what they will do about it. Each student will leave the course with convictions they can stand behind and a guidepost for enacting those commitments in whatever field of work, community participation, or everyday life they imagine themselves engaging with children.

STUDENTS WILL BE ABLE TO
›
Articulate clear, deeply considered beliefs about childhood and children and matching practices, refined over the semester as their thinking develops. (Caring)
›
Reflect on how their own assumptions, experiences, and words shape their stance toward children and childhood. (Human Dimension: self)
›
Through practices developed during the course, support future peers and coworkers in reflecting deeply on their experiences and observations of social and cultural dimensions of childhood. (Human Dimension: others)
›
Continue refining their beliefs and praxes beyond the course, based on, and through, their future experiences with children. (Learning How to Learn)
ANT 324 · COURSE SYLLABUS
Learning from Children: The Politics and Experience of Childhood
Why is it important to listen to children? What can we learn from them?
Course Description

What is childhood? A developmental stage? A biological fact? A culturally specific idea? A social construction that has changed over time? Or a box of tools that we use to describe what it means to be human? How does “childhood” produce specific conditions for people that we call children? How are those conditions related to structures of power? What do the people we call children understand about and do within those conditions?

These are the sorts of questions that frame what scholars call “Childhood Studies.” Each week of this course will introduce new considerations as we try to answer them. Throughout the course, we will see that these questions are not neutral concerns: their answers, and the questions themselves, are contoured by the distinct political stakes of childhood and being a child. Accordingly, as we probe various conditions and experiences of childhood, we will repeatedly ask: What can children teach us about social inequality and oppression? What can children teach us about possibilities for freedom? And what limits do we run into when we try to think and talk about children? In doing so, students will engage deeply with (1) contemporary scholarship on childhood, (2) common considerations in social change and activism (e.g., interdependence, care, dispossession, etc.), and (3) the ways childhood intersects with various social and cultural formations (e.g., Blackness, gender, kinship, immigration, disability, etc.).

Course Format & Pedagogy

This class meets twice a week, for an hour-and-a-half. Our weeks are typically structured around a key concept or problem, and intended to, in working through it, give us a specific way to think about childhood and the people we call children. As we work through these, one simple question underlies each week: so what? In class discussions, we will not simply try to surface what each week adds to or changes in what we think about children, childhood, power, and society. Rather, every discussion section, we will try to answer: what do we do with what we learned?

Your Experiences Matter, Deeply

In fact, not only do they matter, our course and collective learning depends on them. A course about children and childhood can be organized a dozen different ways. We can think about the ways we engage with children in terms of curriculum, pedagogy, care practices, policy, childrearing, cultural practices, healthcare. Through any of these, and so many more, we can make connections to how power operates, how struggles are interconnected, what freedom looks like, and what it might take to achieve it. Organizing this course, and the readings, around conceptual terrain trusts that collectively, we will set the agenda for how precisely this applies to each of us. The only way to achieve that is by sharing our personal experiences and observations. In the words of community organizer maree brown,

“There is a conversation in the room that only these people at this moment can have.”
—  ADRIENNE MAREE BROWN, EMERGENT STRATEGY

We will spend much of our class time building that conversation and staying with it.

Connections Notebook. For these reasons, I highly recommend that you maintain a notebook of connections, observations, and reflections that you make throughout the semester. These might come to you while doing your readings, when volunteering at a community organization that serves children, when working your job, in a quiet moment when you think about your own childhood, listening to a friend or family member talk about their younger siblings or kin, playing with your cousins over Thanksgiving dinner, etc. This is not collected or graded.

Volunteering/Working with Children. Additionally, you are highly encouraged to be actively engaged with children’s lives during the course. The more time you spend with kids, the more opportunities you’ll have to make observations, try out the lenses this course exposes you to, and figure out exactly what they mean for how you interact with childhood and children. Students who are not already regularly interacting with children might consider volunteering at an organization or nonprofit that serves children. You can find a list of such organizations attached to the back of this syllabus or meet with me for specific suggestions and guidance. This is not monitored or assessed.

Readings

This is a discussion-reliant seminar designed for students with some experience thinking and reading about social and cultural contexts. Each class session draws on multiple carefully selected readings. These include academic book chapters, ethnographies, news articles, philosophical writings, and case studies published in journals. Our class discussions, and the kinds of thinking we can do together, hinge on sincere, personal engagement with the assigned readings. I’ve selected readings that provide a conceptual primer: they’ll get you thinking in new ways. Often, readings are paired for you to read across them, and make your own assessments of primary sources with new conceptual tools. You will never be asked to read from a textbook. It is very important that you do your best to read for each class. On nights with multiple readings, a recommended sequence is always provided.

Typically, journaling activities are followed by pair-shares. You will share your writing and thinking with a neighbor, and listen deeply to what they wrote. Sometimes, the pair-share will be accompanied by an additional guiding question or two. During pair-shares, you will get to engage not simply with what each of you thinks about the readings, or the connections you’re making between readings and your lives, but how those connections feel and how they affect you (or don’t).

CLASS DISCUSSIONS

A significant portion of class time is dedicated to whole-class discussions. The agendas for these discussions are set as much by students as they are by the instructor. In setting our agenda for the discussion, students have the opportunity to introduce their reading provocations, which can include media clips. Discussions will typically start from accessible points – a media provocation, a question that directly builds on a pair-share, or one of the guiding questions for the reading. For class sessions following particularly challenging readings, class discussions will typically begin with the goal of building a shared understanding of the reading.

Brief Summary of Assessments

None of the work in this course asks you to write an essay. There is no culminating “research project.” Instead, you’ll engage the material reflexively, through short, intentional writing and through how you show up. Much of this is evaluated through participation, which I treat more expansively than you might be used to. But don’t worry, I provide clear expectations, tips, and feedback. Additionally, each week, you’ll bring one reading provocation, a lingering question or reflection that seeds our discussions. Most classes open with journaling, and some of those writings I collect. Twice in the term, you’ll submit an autoethnographic memo: a brief, first-person reflection connecting a course idea to a specific moment(s) from your own life. Finally, throughout the semester, you’ll cultivate a Beliefs and Praxes ledger, a running set of carefully worded statements about what you believe about childhood and what you intend to do about it, which we’ll sit with together in a one-on-one Dialogue at the end of the course.

WEEK 1

Developmentalism

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Developmental psychology is the main reason childhood looks natural and measurable. It’s what we toss around every day, it’s common sense (which the course must unsettle). So, the course opens inside that view, presenting students with several examples of how developmentalism materializes in the clinical frameworks and policy that underwrite children’s experiences and our perceptions of them. They will recognize many of the familiar terms, categories, criteria, and logic used when we talk about children, design interventions, or track progress: milestones, delays, age-appropriateness, IQ, “on track.” Reading Jenks, students are guided through a careful examination of where those categories come from and what they take for granted. They encounter a scholar’s close analysis and dissection of the theoretical foundations of developmentalism and its conceptual toolbox: Jenks gives them a close reading of Piaget, showing how his account treats the adult as the finished, rational endpoint and the child as an incomplete version of it, and how one particular Western, scientific idea of reason gets installed as the natural and universal goal of growing up.

WEEK 2

The Socially Constructed Child

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Week 1 dismantled childhood’s appearance as a natural, developmental stage. This week, students arrive at a different conclusion: that childhood is made, in different times and places by different people and ideas. They get there in two steps. First, the week unsettles socialization, the idea that children learn their culture and grow into members of society. Students see that idea in Mead’s account of Balinese children, then read Alderson on adultism the same night and see that growing into a member of society still understands children’s lives in terms of becoming adults, and by extension becoming people, instead of understanding children as people in the world. Second, the week shows that there is no single, universal childhood. James, Jenks, and Prout collect the many figures the West has called the child, and Gottlieb sets a different childhood, that of Beng infants, beside them. The pairing invites students to conclude on their own that the child is an idea, something made, and that the developmental child they had taken to be ordinary is one version among many. This is the move that founded childhood studies, and it leads into Week 3, where the West’s particular child, the one mistaken for the universal, gets connected to how our world is structured, and how defining what it means to be human is at the heart of it.

WEEK 3

The Category of Child

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|By now students accept that childhood is constructed. But so what? What are the stakes of that? Week three lays those out, while going one step further. It takes students into the latest wave of childhood studies scholarship, pushing them to see the child as a category of being (a way of deciding who counts as fully human), and subsequently, make connections between that category of being and the structure of the modern world. Students tease out the relationship between the question “what is a child” and the question “what does it mean to be human.” The “aha!” is: the child opens onto, and is entangled with, many forms of domination at once, not the oppression of the young alone and not just a social construct that floats free of power. Childhood studies, as they now know, is founded on the insight that the child is made, not given. This week shows that this making does structural work, entangled with race, colonialism, caste, and property, as one of many toolboxes used to build the modern world. This week closes Part 1 – a densely theoretical venture – squarely on the notion of power and inequality and serves the course’s wager that children have something to teach us about oppression and freedom. This primes students for Part 2, which will take up domains signaled in this week’s readings: reason, possession and dispossession, dependency, social reproduction.

WEEK 4

Reason and Competence

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Week 4 takes students from the theory (that children are positioned as irrational, in-development) down into the machinery that shapes their lives: competency and the law. In familiar realms like consent and the right to vote, students consider how “not yet rational” stops being an idea and starts deciding what a child may do. After glossing the broader patchwork of age-based governance, Day 1 works at the restrictions on children’s authority to make decisions about their own medical treatment, and offers what seems like a “reasonable” argument that lends itself to an easy fix: children understand more than the law admits, so we should prove their reason, advocate for their understanding, and extend their rights. Ready to defend and nuance this argument, students are presented with some of the philosophical leverage for it in John Wall’s argument for children’s suffrage (right to vote). Then, the week pulls the rug out from underneath, showing that in fact, the very idea of reason and consent requires someone to be considered unreasonable, and that’s what the very category of children is tooled for: defining the free, white male propertied citizen. This week directs students into a well-worked path shared across liberation struggles: can the master’s tools dismantle the master’s house? Can arguing children are rational enough to decide things for themselves win them into the club of “human rights?” If voting rights are built on arguing that some people are rational or competent enough to vote, then can a category of people used to define who isn’t rational ever truly be treated as equal? Or will the idea of a “complete human” just adapt? If children can be included in the category of fully human, capable of voting, reasonable, rational, and thus deserving, at what cost? Who must be left as “not human” to keep the definition of “human” strong and solid?

WEEK 5

Innocence

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|This week problematizes innocence. Students first see that some children are granted innocence and others are not, particularly along racial lines: Black children are not read as innocent the way white children are, and so, the readings argue, they are dehumanized and denied childhood’s protections. Class 1 hands students a righteous, well-evidenced conviction: that innocence is a protection unjustly withheld along racial and gendered lines. Students will likely leave wanting to extend innocence to Black children. Then the week turns on the idea of innocence itself. Innocence is not the shelter it appears to be: to be made innocent is to be made a passive, pure object of rescue rather than a knowing, willing person, and it renders the structural causes of children’s endangerment invisible. Because it demands purity, innocence can never be secured for the children who need it most: the moment a child is seen to know or to act, they are cast out of it, which is precisely how Black children are cast out to begin with. The protection of “innocence” students imagined must be extended to Black life now looks like a trap. The week closes by following Ticktin past innocence toward vulnerability, which recognizes need and harm without demanding purity or passivity.

WEEK 6

(Dis)possession

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Are children property? If so, how? With what consequences? What do children do within and against it? Many students will enter with a common sense belief: children belong with their parents, parents are responsible for, and in charge of, their children. Class 1 stress-tests that belief through custody and removal cases without an easy answer; students wrestle with the fact that removal can cause harm, but forced belonging can too. For example, when a court returns a child to a parent she fears. As students adjudicate case studies, they likely do so through the question “Well, who should get to keep the child?” The week problematizes this thought process by probing its assumption. What if the problem (the thing that causes harm in the case studies they read) is not who each child belongs with, but rather the belief that they belong to anyone at all. Through Class 1’s academic reading and discussion, students notice that, in these cases, the question that paints itself as justice—“Where should children go?”—actually produces the child as something that can be placed, handed off, kept — shuttled like an object with value rather than met as a subject with autonomy, caught up in the politics of kinship and the state. Students consider whether childhood, in part, runs on a logic of property vis-à-vis paternalism. The second class demonstrates that the same logic that lets a parent have a child—property—made enslaved people chattel and took Indigenous land. This places students in the heart of contentious debate around shared and overlapping struggles. It isn’t clear whether childhood made the logic of property and race possible, whether the two are homologous, or whether any of that matters against the plain fact that Black children are taken from their families far more often than white children. The week does not answer this for students, and students are expected to, in class and in their Beliefs and Praxes ledger, stake their positions and develop praxes based on them. The week concludes with an understanding that we don’t have all the information about how property interlocks oppressions in children’s daily lives, and we need to turn to their experiences to understand what children actually do inside the system: what are children doing to enact autonomy, or configure collective life, precisely because they cannot hold property themselves and are simultaneously bound by it?

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Students arrive from Week 6 having seen the child constructed as property and dispossessed of personhood, caught in the same logic that made enslaved people chattel and took Indigenous land, with dependency as what follows from being possessed. Social Reproduction

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Who builds and sustains our society? What happens when we start thinking, task by task, event by event, about the labor necessary for our society to operate? Who surfaces, and what are the political stakes and operative assumptions that preclude certain tasks and people from that ledger? Students enter holding a common-sense belief: children don’t build or make our society. The evidence: children are not allowed to labor; school is preparation for work; chores are “helping” tasks granted to children. Children are the ones society spends labor on: the recipients of care and the products of reproduction, not the laborers themselves. The week’s intervention, via social reproduction theory, is that children are positioned within the labor of reproducing society, and that they perform significant portions of that labor themselves, in forms (schoolwork, housework, care) that are devalued, invisibilized, and extracted precisely because “child” and “worker” are constructed as mutually exclusive. Class 1 puts the claim to students directly: is school work? Students inventory their own days growing up (classes attended, homework completed, siblings watched, feelings managed, parents translated for) and test them against social reproduction’s definition of labor as the perpetually necessary work of making and sustaining life. The obvious answer is yes: children perform socially reproductive labor. So a new question emerges: why don’t we see it that way? Who or what stops us from seeing it that way? And what happens if/when we see children’s lives in terms of production? This week challenges students to identify the mechanisms that reassign the value of children’s labor, and to whom that value goes. Adults? Whites? Corporations? Students form connections with other conditions in Part 2: the laws that protect children from work resemble the logic that underwrites innocence; because the only labor that confers standing is the waged kind, children remain dependent no matter how much labor they perform. Students also form connections to broader social struggles, as the reading and in-class media present the argument that care work and women’s domestic labor are a racialized mechanism of value extraction and subordination. The week leaves students inside a materialist version of a question they faced with rights: if children’s labor were recognized — counted, valued, even waged — would that end the extraction or extend its reach? Does protecting children from work shield them, or erase them? Students stake their positions in their ledgers. This week leaves students with a key anthropological/sociological concern in social reproduction: its dual character as extractive and world-making.

PART THREE
What Children Do
PART OVERVIEW|Part 3 turns the course to what children do, and to what we can learn from them. Week 9 primes this undertaking by dismantling inherited vocabularies for children’s action and consequent frameworks for social change, and prepares students to listen for words and worlds not yet imagined. Each week onward immerses students in rich ethnographic accounts of what children actually do — street vending and family work (Estrada, Katz), caregiving in an epidemic (Hunleth), play (imaginal caring, prefigurative claims on the city, fugitive play), escape and world-making inside enclosure (Sojoyner, Shange) — while simultaneously encouraging students to hold the week’s organizing framework skeptically. The question is never just “what are children doing?” or “what does calling it work, care, play, or fugitivity make visible or foreclose?”: it is also “What does this mean for how I act in the world?” Students use accounts of children’s practices as a test of our concepts, attend to what our epistemologies occlude, and let what children do change how we think (Goal 4). Through these weeks, students constantly reimagine their own praxes: their visions of what struggles for liberation can look like, across multiple domains, not children’s alone. The ethnographies provide a necessary granularity that students only otherwise get from (encouraged) outside engagement with children. Through the ethnographic material, students meditate on how their beliefs might translate into actions (or resemble accounts in the texts), and how children’s practices call for specific praxes, forcing them to reconcile their beliefs and ledger statements with what children do. By Week 13, students are drafting their second memo and finalizing ledger statements, so these weeks supply the accounts of children’s lives from which their beliefs and praxes take their final shape.
WEEK 9

Agency & Resistance

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|What happens when we think about what children do as agency or resistance? How are we framing their actions? What rules of the world and definitions of being a person are we assuming? What scripts and conditions are we applying to what it means to act and change the world? What world are we imagining when we say children have agency too, or children are resisting, too? Does this way of thinking sufficiently address the inequalities we’ve identified? Does it give us new ways to think about other social inequalities, or keep us stuck in familiar traps? Students enter Part 3 with staked positions about the conditions of childhood, eager to talk about what young people do within them, and what we can do about them. They arrive thinking with the ready-made frameworks for change: agency and resistance. Children have agency, so make room for it; children are resisting oppression, so learn from their actions and resist the structures of oppression. Week 9 necessarily unsettles these common-sense theories of change and interpretations of action by surfacing their operative assumptions. Students find that agency is definitionally promiscuous, yet reliably defaults to a particular actor (rational, autonomous, strategic), the same liberal subject they watched disqualify children across Parts 1 and 2. How can children have agency when the capacities that define it — reason, autonomy, self-possession — took their shape against the child, their constitutive outside? The child is not simply left out of agency’s definition; the definition was built by leaving the child out. So, what about resistance? Critiques of resistance circulate widely in Black feminist and scholarly literature, but resistance still thrives in social justice communities as a way of naming action and imagining change. Students engage a formative ethnographic account of resistance (Paul Willis’s article-length precursor to Learning to Labor), and look closely at exactly what “resistance” is made to be, and how, in order to uncover its stakes. What is it that kids do that gets read as resistance, what relationship does the word signal, what phenomena are kids’ actions placed in relation to, and how do we know that relation exists? Past the summative thesis (that working-class boys’ oppositional practices and counterculture deliver them to the factory floor, reproducing the oppressive order they oppose), students are pressed to consider new questions. What does it mean for our imagination and understanding to pair an action with a structure of oppression or domination? What does it mean to think of every action only in oppositional terms, and in terms that don’t capture (all) the lived stakes and experiences of children themselves? If resistance remains dialectically tethered to domination, are we confining politics to the horizon of Western state logics? What kind of subject, what notion of being human, does resistance presume? And who is inherently excluded from resisting by way of that presumption? What do these words do to what children do? What do they make visible, and what do they foreclose? The week leaves students interrogating the assumptions beneath any vocabulary for children’s practices, and beneath their own praxes and theories of change. In short, it prepares students to listen for words and worlds not yet imagined, and to read and listen cautiously and critically for the ways their own praxes advance positions from frameworks that presuppose the way the world is before looking at the world, and the people in it.

WEEK 10

Work

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|What should we call what children do? Selling tacos on a Los Angeles sidewalk, herding sheep and hauling water in a Sudanese village. Is it work? Is a working child a childhood interrupted? Students enter Week 10 having accepted, in Week 8, that children perform socially reproductive (and extracted) labor. The working child they picture (the street vendor, the child farmhand) likely remains an emblem of stolen childhood, the figure that child labor law and NGO campaigns are built around, and a trigger for the rescue reflex they learned to notice in themselves in Week 5. Class 1 puts students in front of those children. Estrada’s young vendors describe their work as help, choice, and obligation: “If I don’t help them, who will?” They treat payment as “almost insulting.” They describe a closeness with their parents (Estrada calls it economic empathy) that their non-working siblings and cousins don’t share. The same children run from police, get called “wetback” at school, and mourn their own overwork: “I Work Too MUCH To Be 17.” The reading resurfaces two past problems. First, Estrada writes that children “enact their agency.” What, if anything, is agency, which we disavowed, doing here? And second, children refuse payment because it would turn their relationship into a transaction. So does recognizing children’s work with wages honor it, or undercut its political valence?
Classes 2 and 3 return to Katz’s seminal ethnography of children’s lives in Howa from Week 8. In class two, Chapter 3 forces students to interrogate the binary opposition between work and play. Children work at play, play at work, and produce the village’s environmental knowledge through their braided practices. Class discussion meditates on where this work-play binary comes from. How is it enacted in our own worlds? How do we enact it? What does it do and with what consequence? Chapter 5, a theoretically dense chapter, offers students a new way of thinking about children’s lives: in relation to global political economy. What if the work-play binary is an event? Katz suggests that, as “money becomes the community,” and the Suki Project commodifies wood, water, knowledge, and time itself, work and play are separated for the sake of global political economy at the expense of local knowledge. Chapter 5 invites a tool to connect children’s struggles globally: through political economy and social reproduction. Class discussion provokes a tension: without the word “work,” the extractive violence of capital in children’s everyday practices disappears from view; but with it, the play, relation, and obligation braided through children’s practices all appear circumscribed by the terms (and futures) of capitalism.

WEEK 11

Caregiving

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|What is care? Who can give it? Is care a possible site of political change, or is it how the world maintains its established order? Students enter the week holding care as a moral good. Week 7 gave them interdependence as a commitment shared across liberation struggles, Week 8 showed them care labor as extractive and world-making at once. They likely arrive at week 11 already convinced that children give care. What Hunleth offers is that perhaps care is not one thing, with one operative frame, or one set of political stakes. It can be work, sentiment, obligation and exchange all at once, biomedicine, treatment, and global health. This week dwells on Hunleth’s ethnographic monograph of Zambian children’s caregiving during an HIV/TB epidemic. Class 1’s selections ground students in the daily life and material realities of children in George (a peri-urban compound in Lusaka) and introduce the children’s distinctions of care: keeping (kusunga) versus caring (kusamala); “work” versus “help.” Students notice how each hangs on the relationship implicated, not the task executed; the advocate; the orphan redefined as a child cast away, uncared for, unloved. Care, for these children, is “being closer.” Class one’s selections expose the strains of thinking care as an individuated act: for Zambian children, care necessarily involves proximity as a strategy for social and biological survival. Class 2 unsettles the moralistic frame of care that takes good care as open, honest, earnest, and legible. The selections for class two trace how children’s acts of concealment enact caring relations and attunements. This provokes: how do we even know if care is happening at all? If we can’t know whether or not care is happening, is that because of the terms we’ve set out for what it means for something to happen and effect change (that it must be legible to us?) or is it because care does not have the political valence we assumed it had? Or is it because what children are doing isn’t actually “Care”? Class 2’s discussion raises these questions. And what do we make of children repurposing care regimes of governance like biomedical treatment protocols? The selections include an ethnographic account of children barred from the TB Corner taking the rules and materials of a global treatment protocol and using them to secure belonging. They follow the medicine, monitor dosages, offer their own sputum, “nurtur[ing] dependence through their attempts to create interdependence.” Class 2 lingers with this debate: Is that care as governance? Is it extraction — children’s unwaged work making “rationing by queue” survivable, “optimally benefit[ing]” the very state that gutted the health system? Or is it children making livable lives out of what the epidemic and the state left them? And is that politics, or just survival? The week returns students to a broader thematic quandary: who does recognition serve? As with voting rights and wages: if global health recognized children as caregivers — counted them, designed programs around them — would that recognition be redress, or capture? Is making children’s care visible a politics, or a substitute for one? Does celebrating how resourcefully children care make it easier to leave in place the conditions that force them to?

Play

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Of all the words Part 3 tests, play is the one already assigned to children, and the readiest dismissal of what they do (“just playing”). Students arrive searching for vocabularies and praxes equal to their beliefs, wondering whether play is one. Each reading recharges the word. Class 2 opens play’s political and historical registers. Webster shows play raced as innocence’s twin: white childhood’s property, Black children’s play surveilled, dangerous, and strategically obscured even by their own advocates, leaving it to haunt the archive. Students leave asking whether play can be recognized without being captured, and carry “fugitive play” into Week 13.

WEEK 13

Fugitivity

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|Can education be a site of freedom as much as a site of struggle? Children are forced to spend most of their days in school, doing what others tell them, enmeshed in the carceral state and its violence. What might we learn from their actions? Is it possible to find hope in such enclosed places? Students enter the week understanding schools as sites of social reproduction, for good and for bad. They left Paul Willis uncertain whether “resistance” is adequate or helpful for thinking about what kids do, precisely because it traps them in an interpretive relation with capitalism. What the course has not yet introduced: the relationship between school, the institution that structures children’s daily lives, and carcerality. This week offers students something different on two counts: how and what we look at (toward the minor, the refusals, the withdrawals), and what we read children’s actions in relation with (carcerality and anti-blackness). Class 1 introduces students to enclosed places and Black fugitivity. The reading guide poses: if schooling is flush with carceral, anti-Black violence, what damage are we doing when we reinforce a narrative that Black students should not drop out of school, or advance solutions aimed at retention? The reading models fugitive study: an understanding of Marley, Devon, and P.J.’s disengagements as analyses of schooling, not failures to be schooled. Through rich ethnographic account, students see school is doing precisely what it’s intended to do. Finally, the reading returns students to Willis, whom Sojoyner critiques explicitly by name. If students leave Class 1 thinking “Well, we just need to design progressive, radical schools, grounded in the Black tradition,” Class 2 throws into question the security of progressive reform as a basis for beliefs (like those the students write in their ledger). The chapter from Progressive Dystopia promotes Shange’s central question: “Who loses when we try to win?” Confronted with a portrait of a social-justice school that teaches Fanon and suspends Black students at the most disproportionate rate in San Francisco, students must debate the very premise of winning as they work out their beliefs and praxes. A recurring course theme resurfaces: institutions, ideas, and practices built from critique can themselves become the enclosure. Then, the chapter offers students a different vision of the political, abolition in mundane, ordinary, fugitive, willful defiance — stolen minutes: hallway idleness, song, ratchetry. What does it mean if we think about liberation as “a velocity rather than a state of being”? Class discussion will surface these tensions and speculations, circling challenges like: is reading idleness as abolitionist practice learning from children, or romanticizing them? Can a term, attunement, and practice rooted in the plantation travel to children’s relations within an adultist world? Is our classroom an enclosed place? Can those of us who work in schools ever exceed their carceral and anti-black logics? Where do children’s refusals teach us to look for freedom?

WEEK 14

Relationality and Tying it All Together

SUMMARY & LEARNING NARRATIVE|What should we call what children do? What should we make of the relationships children sustain with kin and fictive mothers, with police officers and each other? Are these simply how children survive the conditions this course has spent a semester mapping, or do they model how a world could be organized? And if the latter, what keeps us from seeing them that way? After five weeks of testing words (work, care, play, fugitivity), students enter Week 14 considering distinct and recurrent affordances and limitations. This week’s ethnography performs the entire course in miniature, and within it, the course’s last framework.
Class 1 immerses students in the relational world of Sealdah station (a major commuter railway station in Calcutta, India): a police billboard urging citizens to deliver out-of-school children, a nation narrating its “arrival” through the schooled child, an NGO shelter making “street children” out of boys on a platform. Students recognize developmental universalism (Week 1), the social construction of the child (Week 2), the colonial contours of the child figure (Week 3), the work of innocence and victimhood (Week 5), all performed knowingly by children themselves in Balagopalan’s ethnographic material. For example, Shankar observes that “the real drama is that we are really acting out the ways people usually think of us” and Rajesh invents a tragic case file because the intake interview demands one. Children are not just negotiating the conditions of childhood, but mobilizing the categorical terms and operative assumptions that produce it. The selections for Class 1 surface all of this within the platform’s relational world: fictive mothers, shared earnings, a “vast circulatory system” of affection in which ethics means loyalty owed to persons. Even maturity becomes relational: children describe themselves as “big,” “small,” or “in the middle” by the responsibilities they carry, not by their stature or wealth.
The selections for Class 2 explicitly explore the politics of this relationality. Jehangir refuses to report the policeman who beat him: “he was doing his job and helping me.” The failure of rights discourse (Week 4) resurfaces in a misreading of Jehangir’s refusal as illiteracy. The reading reworks the term “responsibility” to describe this relational politics: being “called by the other before will,” agency that “does not have to be individuated.” The reading exemplifies how a vocabulary can be built from what children actually do: sending earnings home, sustaining kin real and fictive, inhabiting rights pedagogy with shrewd irony. Selected vignettes challenge students to rethink dependency, social reproduction, work, and caregiving as a single relational fabric. Finally, the reading’s Conclusion connects children’s refusal of khatni to Dalit struggles over manual and mental labor, returning to the course’s operative wager: children’s struggles are deeply tethered to, informed by, and shape broader struggles for liberation.
The course’s final reading challenges students to hold the contradictions of political life, not resolve them: Balagopalan depicts a state reaching for these children’s attunement to responsibility, attempting to conscript it into vocational streaming under the banner of “skill development.” Students might come to see value in holding contradiction: isolated celebration of marginal children’s resilience feeds a compensatory apparatus that naturalizes that resilience and relieves the state/society of its obligations. Class 2’s discussion, accordingly, poses whether the problem is the webs of reciprocity in which children are valued, or that those webs persist inside conditions built on their devaluation. Can change be a matter of disciplining our attention? Does protecting and amplifying the practices children already sustain subject them to governance? How do we keep open “the ethical imperative within universal projects of emancipation”? As students finalize their beliefs and praxes, they are presented with a model for holding the contradictions we all inhabit, in childhood and otherwise.

Participation (40%)

This class is what we, together, make it to be. What each of us gets out of it depends on what we each put in: how we show up, the relationships we build, and the work we do. For that reason, participation makes up a significant portion of the grade, and I assess it rigorously and holistically. This may feel different from how many courses handle participation, so it is worth saying clearly: participation here reaches well beyond whether you spoke during a class discussion, and you will receive plenty of guidance and feedback along the way. The dimensions below name what I am actually looking for.

DIMENSIONS OF PARTICIPATION

I evaluate participation holistically. Alongside how often you take part, I weigh a range of dimensions:

Pair sharing — How active were you during pair-shares? Did you commit to connecting with your partner, work to keep the conversation going, and engage with their ideas?

Small group discussions — Were you active in your small groups? Did you help keep the conversation going, work to include your group mates, and engage with their ideas?

Whole-class discussions — Did you contribute or actively listen? Did you make space and invite others in?

Discussion activities — Did you engage with the activities during discussion section (journaling, lists, charts, and the like)?

Critical engagement — Did you challenge yourself and your thinking during discussion? Did you bring vulnerability and lived experience to bear on the material, when appropriate?

Contributions to the learning community — Did you contribute to building a learning community and demonstrate care for your peers, through conversations, office hours, after-class chats, and the like?

Engagement with instructor — Did you engage with the instructor? Did you reach out when you needed support? Did you share your thinking and experience or provide feedback?

Attendance — Did you attend class, and did you communicate proactively when circumstances necessarily prevented you from attending?

Beliefs and Praxes Ledger (30%)

Throughout the course, you will build and maintain a Beliefs and Praxes Ledger. The Beliefs and Praxes Ledger is a running list of 1–2 sentence, carefully worded, deeply considered statements. The document answers the questions the course keeps returning to: What do you believe about childhood? What can, or should, we — and you — do about it? These beliefs can be abstract or concrete. They are your running ledger of how you are making sense, and making use, of the course’s content: its texts, class discussions, office hours, and whatever else stays with you along the way.

A belief statement is its own kind of writing. Whatever is sticking with you along this journey, think deeply about it, and write it succinctly and specifically. You do not need to cite texts. These statements are not notes on what I have said, what a text has said, or what a peer has said. They are beliefs you stand behind. You select them, among the many thoughts and ideas you have, because they resonate strongly, and you articulate them in a way that comes from how you think about the world.

Your statements are an excellent tool to bring into discussion. Say you walk away from a reading with an idea. Maybe it is something you disagree with, or some sense the reading gives you about how to treat children, or about your own childhood. Write it, carefully and clearly, and bring it up in class.

Across these pieces the ledger is worth 30% of your grade, and I assign that grade holistically. I read both submissions and our end-of-semester Dialogue together, as one developing body of work, attending to the care, depth, and growth they show across the term.

SUBMISSIONS

You submit your ledger at two points in the term. Each one is a snapshot of a living document that keeps changing as your thinking does.

›
Mid-semester submission (5%): At the midpoint of the course, submit a ledger of 10 statements: the beliefs and commitments you most stand behind so far. Aim for care over coverage: ten statements you have weighed and worded with precision. Treat this as a working draft. You will likely cut, combine, compress, or sharpen a third of them as the second half of the course reshapes your thinking and opens room for new beliefs. I read these to understand where your thinking is heading and to respond in kind; I am not marking any belief right or wrong.
›
End-semester submission (10%): In the final stretch of the course, submit a ledger of 15 statements: the version you carry into the Beliefs and Praxes Dialogue. Your thinking has moved by now, and this ledger should show it: keep the statements that still hold, revise the ones that have deepened, and add the beliefs and commitments the second half of the course brought into focus. Choose the words with real care. These are the statements we will sit with together, so let them say exactly what you mean, and let them name both what you believe about childhood and what you intend to do about it.
THE BELIEFS AND PRAXES DIALOGUE (15%)

At the end of the semester, you will set up a one-on-one meeting with me to talk through your ledger together. Plan for about twenty minutes. We will move openly across your statements, and I will invite you to make connections among them and out from them: to the readings, our class discussions, your autoethnographic memos, your own life, and the goals you are carrying forward. The statements you have written give us a place to begin and a guide for where the conversation can go.

I come to this conversation genuinely curious about what you are thinking as you leave the course. I use your statements as a starting point and a reference for my engagement, and I come to learn from you, to think alongside you, and to share my own experiences and questions where they help. I am not weighing whether your beliefs are right or wrong. This is a collaborative conversation. It is not an oral quiz, and it is not a defense.

ASSESSMENT RATIONALE|The Beliefs and Praxes Dialogue is an intentional, relational, and reciprocal close to the course. The course’s goals center on how students think about childhood and what they will do about it; they do not turn on writing polish or argumentation. A final paper, especially one not scaffolded across the term, tends to place an unrealistic burden on students and can overwhelm. The Dialogue lets me work directly with students’ thinking: to engage them, learn from them, and share back. Their ledger makes this possible: building it pushes students to think exceptionally deeply about each belief, to decide which beliefs matter most, and to find the precise words that say exactly what they mean.
Two Autoethnographic Memos (12%)

Twice during the semester, you will submit an Autoethnographic Memo: the first by the end of Week 7, the second by the end of Week 13 (6% each). A memo is a first-person reflection, 500 words at most, on a single idea, concept, problem, challenge, or lingering question from the course.

Every memo does one essential thing: it draws a specific connection between the course and your own life. Start with something that has stayed with you (a concept that won’t settle, an argument you keep arguing with, a question that followed you out of the room) and bring it together with a particular observation or event from your life, present or past: a moment from your own childhood, something you witnessed at work or while volunteering, a conversation with a younger sibling or cousin, something you noticed at a family gathering or on the bus. The more specific the moment, the more your reflection has to work with. If you have been keeping a Connections Notebook, you already hold a running list of starting points.

This is a personal essay, and it should say something. Your memo explores a thesis (a main point, a unifying claim, a problem you are working out) and makes its case through your own life. Engage at least one reading directly: quote it, tease out its ideas and images, and place them in conversation with your own scenes and observations. What I want to read is the dialogue happening in your head between your life and the course. The form that dialogue takes is open. Your memo might be a collection of scenes weaving around a problem, a single sustained analysis of one event or moment, a photo essay with annotations, a letter to your younger self or to a child in your life, an artifact from your own childhood (a photograph, a report card, a diary page) annotated and read closely, a comic or graphic essay, or another mode of communicative expression. Whatever form you choose, write in the first person, and include enough writing for me to follow the point you are making.

One criterion matters above all others: how, and whether, you care about what you wrote. I read each memo for the quality of your engagement with the course: Do you make yourself vulnerable on the page? Do you hold yourself and your thoughts accountable? Are you open to transforming, and being transformed by, your understanding of children and childhood? Do you read closely and think personally about what an author writes, or about the stories of children you have read? Whatever its style, a piece of writing works when it feels like you are risking something: when you let an idea or reading touch a real moment in your life and follow what happens earnestly. Think of the feeling you had when submitting a college application essay you were particularly proud of, or when you sent a letter to someone you cared deeply about. That is the feeling you’re after when writing this memo.

On weeks when you submit a memo, you do not need to submit a reading provocation.

ASSESSMENT RATIONALE|The memos put two of the course’s goals into an assessable form: relating course ideas to one’s own observations and lived experience (Integration), and reflecting on how one’s own assumptions and experiences shape one’s stance toward children (Human Dimension). They also feed the course’s culminating work, the Beliefs and Praxes Ledger and Dialogue. The assignment is designed as AI-resistant. Its central requirement, a specific observation or event from the student’s own life brought into contact with a course concept, is challenging for a large language model to fabricate. The instruction to write in first person attempts to make this more accessible and surface distinct authorial voices (we tend to really write ourselves into our voice when writing about ourselves). Care, rather than polish, governs the assessment because the course’s outcomes depend on students letting the material work on them. The two deadlines, as Parts 2 and 3 near their close, ask students to consolidate each part of the course in personal terms before it moves on.

Weekly Reading Provocations (8%)

Each week, you’ll bring one reading provocation, submitted at least one hour before either the first or second class. These should reflect what you would like to spend class time discussing; they should be composed with consideration for what they would contribute to our community’s learning. There are two possible venues for submitting your Reading Provocation:

1.
Canvas Discussion Post - Lingering question or reflection: Submit one or two carefully written lingering questions, or a short reflection (80 words max), to the given class sessions’ Canvas discussion forum. A helpful guide for developing and writing these lives at the top of each Canvas discussion post page for each class session. A lingering question might be a genuine point of confusion you want to take up with a classmate, a tension you noticed in the reading, or something the reading stirred up that you cannot quite let go of.
2.
Instagram post: Create a post on the class Instagram account. Posts can vary in style, content, and length. One provocation a week is plenty. One provocation per week, not one per class session. On weeks when you submit an autoethnographic memo, you do not need to submit a reading provocation.

Each reading provocation will receive one of three possible marks:

✓

✓–

No submission or provocation does not demonstrate engagement with reading. (0/10 points)

LATE WORK

Outside of the readings, this is not a deadline-intensive course. Neither meeting deadlines nor completing work under time constraints is a key learning outcome for this course. However, deadlines have been set to ensure you continue along the learning journey I have mapped for this course and alongside your peers.
